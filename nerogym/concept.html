What the gym is for

Eray's idea is a gym that fixes what commercial gyms get wrong, run under the Nero name, using NeroPay's own software. It sits inside a longer plan: NeroPay, NeroGym, and later Nero Construction, Nero Tech, Nero Software.

Three things a physical gym would do for the group, in order of how much they are worth:

  1. A live reference for NeroGym, the software. The gym and PT management product ships free with a merchant account and today has no visible customer. A site we run is proof that it works at scale, with our own data to show.
  2. A business that stands on its own. A 5,000 sq ft strength gym at £49.99 clears about £13,600 a month in running costs at roughly 210 members and returns its capital in year four or five. That is a real business, not a marketing line, and the model page shows the downside as well.
  3. A brand people see every day. A lit N on an industrial unit on the A560 is seen by more of Stockport in a week than every NeroPay post this year. It costs nothing extra once the gym exists.

The cheaper way to get item one

If the software reference is the main point, there is a version that costs a fraction of the build: partner with an existing independent gym in Stockport, give them the Nero software and the door hardware, share the content rights and publish a named case study. No planning risk, no lease, no £400,000. That option has not been priced. It should be, before the build is chosen, and it is the first question for Eray on the overview page.

Who it is for

The original brief said 16–35: students, young professionals, creators. Stockport's numbers point somewhere slightly older. The borough loses its 18–24s to university and the city centre and gets them back at 28–35; its 20–24 band is the smallest adult cohort it has. The 25–34 share in the Stockport constituency is 15.6% against 13.3% for the region. So:

Core, who pay the bills
27–40, working, living within a ten-minute drive. They have trained before, they lift, they have left PureGym or Life Leisure once already, and they will pay £15 a month more for a room that is not crowded at 6pm and where the cable machine gets fixed. They join on Direct Debit and stay 18–24 months if the place is looked after.
Second, who fill the daytime
18–26 students and early-career. Price-sensitive, phone-first, the ones who post. They take the off-peak or student tier and they are the reason the creator infrastructure exists. They churn faster (one in ten quit within 90 days, sector-wide) and they are why the model uses 4.5% monthly churn rather than something kinder.
Third, who fill the afternoon
Staff at employers within walking distance. Corporate memberships at a discount, sold to HR in the estate next door. Only works if the unit sits at the seam between an employment cluster and housing, which is a premises criterion, not a marketing one.
Not a target
Under-16s in any form; 16–17s only in staffed hours with a parental form. Not people who want classes, a pool or a spa. Not anyone we would need to undercut Foundry to win.

What the research did not find: any UK evidence that members choose a gym on its aesthetics. The substantiated design driver is different and less glamorous. 17% of women report gym intimidation, up 7 points year on year, and crowding and slow repairs are the top complaints in the Which? survey of 6,498 members. The look earns the first visit. Space, upkeep and a lack of embarrassment earn month eighteen.

How it is different, in things we can actually promise

Each of these is a commitment a member could hold us to, not an adjective. Each one costs money and the cost is in the ledger.

1 · Kit that works, and enough of it

Eray's thesis was that commercial gyms neglect their equipment. The evidence says the chains are outspending everyone on kit (PureGym £177m capex in FY25, JD Gyms £21m on refurbishment in one year) but they are slow to repair and they let queues form on the popular stations. So the promise is operational: any fault fixed or the station replaced within 48 hours, a spare-parts shelf on site, and two of everything people queue for: two cable stacks, two leg presses, two hip thrust set-ups, eight racks for 5,000 sq ft. We publish the uptime.

2 · Space per member, and a cap

Budget chains run about 3.3 sq ft per member. We run ten. At 5,000 sq ft that means membership capped at roughly 480–500, and we say so. A waiting list is a marketing asset and a capacity promise at once. It also means the price has to be £49.99 in the core tier, not £29.99, because the model does not close at 500 members on £29.99.

3 · A room built for the camera, without turning the floor into a set

Indirect warm light, oak and dark tone materials, no cave and no operating theatre. Filming is allowed in designated creator zones and nowhere else on the floor. That is the privacy answer, and it is the opposite of what a "content creator gym" slogan implies, which is why the slogan is dropped. Detail below.

4 · The software does what the front desk does at other gyms

One app: join, pay, enter by QR, book a PT or a massage chair, tap a machine to log it, cancel in two taps. No card, no fob, no phone queue to leave. No £25 failed-payment fee. The incumbents' collection agents charge that to the member; we absorb a retry instead. It is the one line of the business where being a payments company is an actual advantage.

5 · Staffed when it matters, safe when it is not

Staffed 06:00–21:00 weekdays in the mid model, unstaffed overnight with monitored CCTV, panic lanyards, a signed and registered defibrillator and doors that open from the inside with one push. The Henry Best inquest set the standard for what an unstaffed gym owes its members. We design to it rather than argue with it.

The concession, which we make in every educational piece we publish: someone who wants classes, a pool, a sauna and a crèche for under £60 a month should join Life Leisure. Someone who trains at 5am twice a week for £20 should stay at PureGym. We are for the person who lifts four times a week and is tired of waiting for a rack.

Design and lighting

The reference points are a boutique hotel lobby, an Apple Store's restraint and a modern café, not a bodybuilding gym and not a white commercial box. Everything below is specifiable to a contractor and priced in the one-off ledger.

Palette
Charcoal walls where kit sits against them (so the floor reads as one calm surface), off-white elsewhere, oak slat feature walls at reception and the lounge, matte black steelwork. Yellow only as the accent it is for NeroPay: the N, the door reveal, the wayfinding. No red, no neon strip along every wall.
Floor
Black rubber tile throughout the training floor, 20mm under machines and 40–50mm under the racks and platforms. Polished concrete in reception and the lounge if the slab allows it, engineered oak if it does not.
Ceiling
Exposed and painted dark, services visible and tidy. A high-bay industrial unit is an asset here; that is why an E(g) light-industrial unit is the target, not an office.
Light levels
Training floor 300–400 lux at 3500–4000K from indirect linear LED and uplight, so faces are lit from the front and not from a downlight above. Racks get a second, warmer layer at 3000K. Recovery and lounge at 150–200 lux, 2700K. Reception has one statement fitting. Everything dimmable on a scene controller so the room changes at 9pm.
Acoustics
Industrial units echo. Acoustic baffles above the free-weight zone and rubber underlay under the platforms. The music is zoned so the lounge is quiet.
Signage
One large illuminated N on the fascia, the NeroGym lockup at the door, and wayfinding in the Chivo we already use. Nothing else on the outside.
Avoid
Pure black interiors, exposed LED strips, motivational quotes on walls, branded props, anything glossy. The terminal rule from NeroPay's brand applies in spirit: the kit is the only object in the room that needs to look like it belongs to a brand.

Creator infrastructure, with the privacy answer

UK gyms have been restricting filming since 2024, after members complained about being in the background of strangers' videos. PureGym, The Gym Group and JD Gyms all now publish filming rules. A gym that advertises itself as a creator gym has to answer the question "will I end up in someone's TikTok?" before it answers anything else. Our answer:

  • Two creator zones, not a creator gym. Each is a rack plus a bench in a corner, with a phone shelf, a mount, a warm key light and a dark matte backdrop, screened from the main floor by an oak slat wall. Filming happens inside the zone, facing the backdrop, and nowhere else. Bookable in the app in 20-minute slots, free for members.
  • A signature wall at reception. The illuminated N, a bench and good light. The one place a photo is expected. It is the free marketing the brief wanted and it is nowhere near anyone training.
  • No filming on the main floor, ever, and no phones in the changing rooms. Written into the terms, on the wall, and enforced. This is the rule members actually want.
  • A creator programme, not free memberships. Ten local creators at launch on a six-month agreement: they get the zone booked out on Sunday mornings and a member rate; we get a tag in every post and the right to reuse. Priced on the marketing page.
  • Halo mirrors and ring lights are decoration. The brief lists them. They photograph well on day one and look like a salon by month six. Do the lighting properly instead and skip them.

Digital screens

Screens earn their place where people stand still. On a training floor they are noise. Five positions, each with an idle state and an active state, all on one signage account we run ourselves.

  • Reception1 × 55" + 1 × 32" touch

    Idle: the gym in use, the live occupancy number, this week's uptime. Touch: join, book a PT, book a creator slot, ask for help. Returns to idle after 60 seconds.

  • Water station1 × 43"

    The only place a member stands for 40 seconds with nothing to do. Monthly challenge, PT availability, the recovery menu, one partner slot. No sound.

  • Recovery zone1 × 43"

    Stretch sequences, breathing timers, how to use the chairs and the boots. Quiet content, warm palette, no adverts.

  • Lounge1 × 55"

    Community board: challenge leaderboards by attendance not by weight, member milestones people have opted into, what's on. Nothing that could embarrass anyone.

  • Entrance corridor1 × 32" portrait

    The house rules, the filming rule, the occupancy count and the emergency information. Legally useful and it makes the rules feel like design rather than a laminated sign.

On advertising revenue: gym screen networks exist in the UK, but they pay operators very little or supply screens free in exchange for the inventory, and we found no published rate a 500-member independent could rely on. Treat screen advertising as £0 in the model and a local-partner upside (the physio next door, a meal-prep company) once the room has footfall.

Software roadmap

This is the part of the plan that is genuinely ours to build and the part that eats Eray's time. The Germany launch and the investment round come first; the phases below are ordered so that nothing has to exist before the lease is signed, and Phase 1 is exactly what NeroGym the product needs to sell to anyone else.

Phase 1 · before opening: membership and the door

  • Join online in under three minutes: tier, Direct Debit mandate, photo, terms. Under-18 branch with a guardian form.
  • QR entry: rotating code in the app, reader on a single-leaf door with a fail-safe maglock and an anti-tailgate camera, rather than a turnstile. Speed gates cost more than the rest of the door together and they photograph badly.
  • Membership admin: freeze, cancel, upgrade, arrears with a soft retry and no member-facing penalty. A staff view on the reception tablet.
  • Bookings: PT slots, creator zone, massage chairs and boots, all as calendar resources with a per-slot price.
  • Payments: Direct Debit for memberships via a Bacs bureau or GoCardless behind our interface, cards on the counter for everything else.

Phase 2 · months 3–9: the machine log

  • An NFC tag and a printed QR on every station, about 60 tags. On-metal NTAG213 tags cost pence each; the engraving is the expensive part. Tap logs machine, time and date; a second tap closes the set. iPhones read tags in the background with an App Clip, Android via the app.
  • A workout history the member can see, and a weekly summary that says what it saw: "chest twice, no pulling movements". Rules first, a model later; a rule that says "you haven't been for 14 days" is the churn tool, because members cancel on average five months after their last visit and the attendance data knows before the Direct Debit file does.
  • Station-level utilisation for us: which kit queues, which sits idle, which to duplicate. This is the data that makes the 48-hour promise cheap to keep.

Phase 3 · year 2: community and the second site

  • Attendance challenges, streaks, opt-in milestones on the lounge screen. Referral credits inside the app.
  • Personalisation from the log: suggested sessions, quieter-time nudges from live occupancy.
  • Multi-site membership, because the product is only worth selling to other operators once it has run one site for a year without the founder in the building.

Attention is the named risk, not money. NeroPay has one engineer. Building Phase 1 before the gym decision is made is the exact cost that does not appear anywhere in this model. If it goes ahead early, scope it as the demo for the software product, which has no reference customer, not as gym infrastructure.

Recovery, the lounge and food

Recovery, dry and phased
Compression boots and a red-light panel need a plug socket and cost under £10,000 together. A sauna brings showers, drainage and a water-treatment regime with it; a cold plunge brings a filtration burden nobody wants to own at 5am. PureGym went touchless for the same reasons and is trialling exactly this six miles away at Urban Exchange for £6–8 a month. Ours is included in the top tier and £8 a month as an add-on below it.
Massage chairs
Nobody in the UK publishes utilisation. Buy one commercial chair (about £1,600–£3,500), card reader not coins, price it at £3 for 10 minutes, and measure for 90 days before buying the second. The business case rests on an attach rate that does not exist in the public domain, so make the number ourselves.
Lounge
Six seats, a long bench, plants, the community screen, the good coffee machine. Small on purpose; it is somewhere to wait for a lift and finish a shake, not a café. Modern café in feel, not in menu.
Food and drink
Phase 1 is two operator-supplied vending machines (drinks, protein snacks, a shake machine) on a free-on-loan deal. £60 a month of commission for zero capital beats owning machines that need someone to fill them. Phase 2, once footfall is known, is a supplement partner in the lounge on a revenue share.
Personal trainers
Six licensed PTs at a fixed monthly rent, no rostered shifts, no price-setting by us and a genuine right of substitution, on an agreement drafted by an employment solicitor. UK comparables run £250–£900 a month; a PT billing twenty Manchester sessions a week grosses about £3,200, so £300 is under 10% of their turnover. This is the strongest ancillary line and the only one whose legal downside exceeds its revenue if it is done casually.

What must be in place for this to work

In order. If one of these is missing the ones after it do not matter.

  1. Eray's three answers. Gym or software showcase; how much capital and from where; before or after the round closes. Nothing below is worth spending on until these are in writing.
  2. A unit that passes the premises gate. Class E lawful use or a granted change of use, 4,500–6,000 sq ft, clear height, parking, three-phase power, drainage for a changing block, at the seam of jobs and homes. The premises page is the checklist.
  3. Capital of roughly plus the deposit and the first months' rent, held before the lease is signed, not raised against it. The worst cash position in the ramp is around month four.
  4. A named general manager who is in the building. Gyms that work are run by someone who is there. This is a hire, not a founder's side project.
  5. Phase 1 software live and tested on the door six weeks before opening, with a fallback (a plain fob reader) in the cupboard.
  6. The compliance stack signed off: fire risk assessment recorded, Approved Document T toilets, means of escape checked against the access control, AED registered on The Circuit, PT agreements drafted, membership terms compliant with the Ashbourne judgment.
  7. A pre-sale of at least 250 founding members before the doors open, at a locked founder price. The ramp only works if the room is a third full on day one.
  8. The 48-hour repair promise resourced: a maintenance contract, a parts shelf and a duplicate of every high-demand station in the opening kit list.
